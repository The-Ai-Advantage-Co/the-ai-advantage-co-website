What is Deep Research?

Deep Research is Gemini's autonomous research agent. Unlike a standard web search, it doesn't return a list of links — it reads dozens of sources, synthesises findings, and delivers a fully structured report with inline citations, all without you having to click anything.

It's available in Gemini Advanced (1.5 Pro and 2.0 models) and is designed for complex questions that would normally take hours of reading to answer properly: competitive analysis, academic topic overviews, market research, policy summaries, and technical deep-dives.

🔍 Multi-step searching

Runs 20–100+ individual web searches, following leads across sources to build a complete picture.

📄 Cited reports

Every claim links back to a specific source, so you can verify and explore further.

⏱ Research in minutes

Tasks that take human researchers hours complete in 5–15 minutes.

📥 Export to Docs

Send the finished report directly to Google Docs for editing, sharing, and collaboration.

Who it's for:

Professionals, researchers, students, and business owners who need reliable, comprehensive information fast — without reading 40 browser tabs.

How Deep Research Works

Deep Research works in two phases: planning and execution. Understanding both phases helps you write better queries and interpret results accurately.

Phase 1 — Research Plan

When you submit a Deep Research query, Gemini first generates a research plan — a bullet-point outline of the sub-questions it intends to answer. You can review and edit this plan before research begins. This is your opportunity to redirect, narrow, or expand the scope.

1

You submit the query

Type your research question in the Gemini chat interface and select "Deep Research" mode.

2

Gemini generates a plan

A structured outline appears showing what it will research. Review and edit as needed.

3

Autonomous research begins

Gemini searches the web iteratively, reads pages, follows sources, and builds its knowledge base.

4

Report is delivered

A fully structured report with headings, bullet points, and inline citations appears in chat.

Edit the plan:

The research plan stage is underused. If you see the plan missing a key angle — competitor, geography, time period — add it before confirming. You'll get a much better report.

What it searches

Deep Research uses Google Search and reads the full text of web pages, PDFs, and documents it finds. It does not currently read content behind paywalls or from your private Google Drive files.

Crafting the Right Query

Deep Research works best with specific, scoped questions — not vague topics. The more context you provide, the more targeted the research plan and the more relevant the final report.

Query TypeWeak ExampleStrong Example
Business"Tell me about the EV market""Compare the top 5 EV manufacturers by global market share in 2024, focusing on revenue, charging infrastructure, and fleet partnerships"
Academic"What is ADHD?""Summarise the current research on ADHD in adults, including diagnosis rates, treatment options, and workplace accommodations, from 2020–2024"
Competitor"Tell me about my competitors""Research the top 3 B2B SaaS CRM platforms competing with Salesforce: HubSpot, Pipedrive, and Zoho. Cover pricing, features, and target segments."
Policy"AI regulation""Summarise the key provisions of the EU AI Act as it applies to high-risk AI systems in healthcare, including compliance timelines and penalties."
The scope formula:

Topic + specific angle + time period or geography + what you want to do with the information. Example: "Research [topic] focusing on [angle] in [period/region] to help me [purpose]."

Query add-ons that improve output

  • "Include statistics and data where available" — gets numerical evidence rather than just commentary
  • "Focus on [year range]" — prevents outdated content from dominating
  • "Exclude [company/topic]" — useful when you already know certain sources well
  • "Structure the report with sections for [X, Y, Z]" — shapes the report outline directly

Reading & Using Reports

A Deep Research report can be 2,000–8,000 words long. Knowing how to navigate and use the report efficiently is as important as knowing how to request one.

Report structure

Reports follow a consistent structure: executive summary → thematic sections → conclusion → sources list. The inline citations are numbered superscripts linking to the sources panel.

📋 Executive summary

Read this first — it gives you the 3–5 key findings. Often this is all you need for a quick brief.

📑 Thematic sections

Each section covers one angle from the research plan. Jump to the sections most relevant to your use case.

🔗 Inline citations

Click any citation number to see the source. Use these to dig deeper into any specific claim.

📊 Sources panel

The full list of sources used. Review this to assess the breadth and quality of the research.

Exporting and using the report

1

Export to Google Docs

Click "Export to Docs" to create a fully editable Google Doc — with all headings and citations intact.

2

Follow up in chat

Ask Gemini to expand a specific section, rewrite it for a different audience, or pull out just the key statistics.

3

Combine with other tools

Paste the report into a Google Slides deck, use it as context for a Gem, or share via Gmail as a briefing.

Follow-up prompts that add value:

After the report is delivered, try: "Summarise the top 5 takeaways as a bullet list" or "Rewrite the executive summary for a non-technical audience" or "What are the most surprising findings in this report?"

Source Evaluation

Deep Research is powerful, but it can only synthesise what's available on the public web. Understanding how to evaluate the sources it uses prevents you from relying on low-quality information.

What makes a good source in a Deep Research report?

SignalGoodCaution
PublisherGovernment sites, universities, major news outlets, peer-reviewed journalsPersonal blogs, single-author sites, forums
RecencyPublished within the last 2 years for fast-moving topicsArticles from 5+ years ago on technology or market topics
SpecificityData-backed claims with named studies or statisticsVague assertions without sourced figures
DiversityMultiple independent sources corroborating the same findingOne source repeated multiple times
Always verify high-stakes claims:

Deep Research does an excellent job of synthesis, but it can occasionally misrepresent a source or include outdated data. For any claim you plan to act on or publish, click through to the original source and verify directly.

Prompting for better sources

  • Add "prioritise peer-reviewed or government sources" to your query
  • Specify "from [year] onwards" to avoid outdated research
  • Ask for "statistics with named sources" to force attribution
  • After the report: "Which of these findings are supported by multiple sources?"

Research Strategies

Deep Research isn't just for one-off questions. Used strategically, it becomes a repeatable research workflow for your role.

Strategy 1 — Competitive intelligence sprint

Run one Deep Research session per competitor per quarter. Use the same query template each time so reports are comparable. Export to Docs, then use Gemini in Docs to generate a competitive comparison table from all reports.

Strategy 2 — Topic mastery onboarding

When entering a new field or project, start with a broad "overview of [topic]" report, then run 3–5 narrower follow-up queries on the most important sub-topics the overview surfaced. Within an afternoon you have deep background on a field that would otherwise take weeks to read.

Strategy 3 — Evidence gathering for proposals

Before writing a business case, use Deep Research to gather supporting statistics and precedents. Ask specifically: "Find evidence that [investment/initiative] has delivered ROI for similar organisations." Then use the citations in your proposal directly.

Stack with Gems:

Create a Gem with your research report as context (paste it into the system instructions). The Gem then becomes a subject-matter expert on that topic — able to answer follow-up questions, draft content, and give advice grounded in the report.

Strategy 4 — Newsletter and content research

Content creators can use Deep Research to rapidly understand a topic before writing. Run the research, export to Docs, then ask Gemini to draft a post, newsletter section, or script based on the findings.

Advanced Techniques

Chain multiple research sessions

Run a broad research session first to identify the key themes, then run targeted sessions on each theme. The second wave of reports will be far more precise because you now know what to ask.

Edit the research plan aggressively

The research plan is shown before research begins. Remove sections that aren't relevant, add specific companies or data points you want covered, and reorder sections to match how you'll use the report. A 2-minute plan review saves significant post-processing.

Use Deep Research as a fact-checking tool

If you have an existing document with claims you're unsure about, ask: "Research whether the following statistics are accurate and find the original sources: [paste claims]." Gemini will search for corroboration or contradictions.

Set a scope boundary explicitly

Deep Research can go wide. If you want depth over breadth, say: "Focus only on academic research, not industry commentary" or "Limit this to the US market." Scope constraints prevent the report from becoming unfocused.

Synthesis prompt after research:

Once the report is delivered, ask: "Based on this research, what are the 3 most important things someone in [my role] should do differently?" This turns a descriptive report into an actionable brief.

Combine Deep Research with NotebookLM

Export the report to Google Docs, then add that Doc to a NotebookLM notebook. NotebookLM can then run Q&A, generate audio overviews, and create study guides from the research — turning a single report into a multi-format knowledge resource.

Suggested Prompts

Competitive Analysis
"Research the top 5 competitors to [my company/product] in [market]. For each, cover: key differentiators, pricing model, target customer, recent product changes, and customer sentiment from reviews. Highlight where they are weakest."
Market Opportunity
"Research the [industry] market in [region/country] focusing on: current market size, growth projections to 2027, key trends, regulatory environment, and which customer segments are underserved. Prioritise data from 2022–2024."
Evidence for Proposal
"Research evidence that [initiative — e.g. flexible working / AI adoption / sustainability investment] has delivered measurable business outcomes for companies similar to [my company size/sector]. Find statistics, case studies, and named examples with sources."
Topic Mastery
"Give me a comprehensive overview of [topic] including: history and key milestones, current state of knowledge, main schools of thought or competing approaches, open questions, and recommended resources for deeper study."
Trend Analysis
"Research the key trends shaping [field] in 2024–2025. For each trend, explain: what's driving it, who is affected, what early signals indicate, and what the implications are for [my role/industry]. Include quantitative data where available."
Technology Evaluation
"Research [technology/tool] for use in [context]. Cover: how it works, key vendors and pricing, real-world implementation examples, known limitations or risks, and how it compares to [alternative approach]. I want to make a procurement decision."
