The Sensitive-Writing Brief
What to tell Claude before any draft
  • Who is the message from (you, your boss, the company)
  • Who is the primary audience (one person, a team, the public)
  • Who else will read it (lawyers, press, investors, family)
  • What's the underlying truth that the message can't contradict
  • What outcome are you trying to produce — specifically, not vaguely
  • What's off-limits — what must NOT be said
📝 THE UNDER-USED 'WHAT'S OFF-LIMITS' FIELD
Telling Claude what NOT to say is often more useful than telling it what TO say. It constrains output away from common cliches and corporate boilerplate that would otherwise creep in. 'Don't use 'unfortunately'; don't soften the decision' produces dramatically cleaner letters.

The brief is the single biggest determinant of quality for sensitive writing. Generic prompts produce generic drafts; specific briefs produce drafts that genuinely capture what you're trying to do. 03
Termination and Difficult-Conversation Letters
The hardest writing in business

For these, the rule is: clear about the decision, kind about the person, exact about the next steps. Claude is good at all three. Always reviewed by HR/legal before sending.

  • State the decision early and unambiguously
  • Acknowledge the person without softening the decision
  • Cover next steps concretely (final day, handover, references, support)
  • Avoid metaphors, hedging, and corporate cliché — they read as cowardice

✅ DO THIS

  • Lead with the decision — clarity is kinder than buildup
  • Acknowledge the person specifically — generic statements feel cold
  • Cover next steps exhaustively — uncertainty compounds distress
  • Offer concrete support (references, transition, EAP)
  • Always have HR/legal review before sending
  • Send by your preferred channel; provide a follow-up conversation option

❌ AVOID THIS

  • Buildup before the decision — reads as cowardice
  • Soft-pedalling ('regretfully', 'unfortunately') — the person reads through it
  • Metaphors and corporate cliché — undermines the seriousness
  • Letters longer than 1 page — every additional paragraph dilutes clarity
  • Personal anger or defensiveness — never appropriate
  • Sending without legal review on anything with regulatory weight

These letters are where Claude's tone-handling matters most. The pattern: clear about the decision, kind about the person, exact about next steps. Always reviewed by HR/legal before sending. The cost of the wrong tone here is human, not commercial — these letters land in someone's worst day.

The structure that consistently works

  • Open: state the decision in the first 1-2 sentences. No buildup.
  • Acknowledge: 1 sentence acknowledging the person without softening the decision.
  • Reason: 1-2 sentences on why, if appropriate to share.
  • Next steps: concrete, dated, exhaustive.
  • Support: what's available (references, EAP, transition support).
  • Close: brief, warm, not effusive.

Public Apologies
Where most go wrong

The internet's best-written apologies share four traits: own the action, not just the impact; specify what's being done differently; don't blame circumstances; close with a path forward.

  • Action-focused ('we shipped a feature that did X'), not impact-only ('we know some users were affected')
  • Specific remediation ('we've reverted the change and will do Y')
  • No 'sorry-if' constructions — say what you did wrong
  • Path forward — concrete next step, not vague intention

The four traits of apologies that work

The internet's best-written apologies share four traits. The internet's worst share opposites. Both patterns are easy to spot once you know them.

  • Own the action — 'we shipped X' beats 'X happened'
  • Specify remediation — concrete next step, not vague intention
  • No 'sorry-if' constructions — say what you did wrong
  • Brief — long apologies read as defensive padding
  • No blame-shifting — even subtle ('our partners did X')
  • Lead with the impact — what you caused, before what you'll do

Investor and Stakeholder Updates
When the news isn't great

Bad updates that work share a pattern: lead with the headline, explain what happened (with the actual numbers), what you're doing, what you're not doing, and when the next data point is. Resist hopium.

  • Lead with the number, not the framing
  • Show your working — investors can spot a lack of honesty in seconds
  • Distinguish between things you control and things you don't
  • End with a specific next-data-point date, not vague optimism
📊 WHEN THE NEWS ISN'T GREAT
Bad updates that work share a pattern: lead with the headline, explain what happened (with the actual numbers), what you're doing, what you're not doing, and when the next data point is. Resist hopium. Investors spot it in seconds.

✅ DO THIS

  • Lead with the headline number — not the framing
  • Show your working — investors spot a lack of honesty
  • Distinguish things you control from things you don't
  • End with a specific next-data-point date
  • Acknowledge what you don't know — uncertainty is more credible than false certainty
  • Keep to 1-2 pages — investors scan, not read

❌ AVOID THIS

  • Burying the number in framing
  • Hopium ('we expect strong rebound') without evidence
  • Vague timelines ('soon', 'in the coming quarter')
  • Generic 'macro conditions' framing — investors discount this heavily
  • Hyperbole ('unprecedented', 'transformative quarter')
  • Missing the next-data-point commitment — investors notice

Regulatory and Compliance Communications
Where every word might be read in court

For regulatory disclosures, customer notifications about incidents, or anything with legal weight, Claude is the drafter; your lawyers are the editors. The pattern: Claude produces a structured, clear, accurate first draft from the facts; legal adjusts for risk.

For anything with regulatory, contractual, or litigation implications, Claude is a drafter, not a publisher. Always reviewed by counsel. State the facts; let counsel decide the framing.

Cultural and Cross-Border Considerations
What flat translation misses

For multinational comms, translation isn't enough — register, formality, and hierarchy conventions differ. Ask Claude to adapt for the audience, not just translate.

  • 'Adapt this for a Japanese executive audience — appropriate formality, indirectness where called for'
  • 'Rewrite for a French B2B audience — they expect more structured argumentation than US/UK'
  • 'Translate to German with the formality appropriate for board-level communication'

For multinational communications, translation isn't enough — register, formality, and hierarchy conventions differ. Direct translation often produces messages that read as rude or naive in the target culture. Claude is meaningfully better than mainstream alternatives at adapting (not just translating) for cultural context.

Common requests: 'Adapt this for a Japanese executive audience — appropriate formality, indirectness where called for', 'Rewrite for a French B2B audience — they expect more structured argumentation than US/UK', 'Translate to German with the formality appropriate for board-level communication'.

What Claude Won't Do
And shouldn't
  • Won't write deliberately misleading communications
  • Won't draft messaging designed to manipulate vulnerable audiences
  • Won't generate content that misrepresents legal obligations
  • Will push back if asked to soften factual statements past the point of accuracy

Honest about its limits

Claude is designed to be helpful, honest, and harmless. For sensitive writing, that means it pushes back on certain framings even when explicitly asked.

  • Won't write deliberately misleading communications
  • Won't draft messaging designed to manipulate vulnerable audiences
  • Won't generate content that misrepresents legal obligations
  • Will push back if asked to soften factual statements past accuracy
  • Will refuse to draft personal communications you don't have authority to send

Common Mistakes & Fixes
What goes wrong — and how to avoid it

Most AI workflows that 'don't work' fall into one of these patterns. The fix is almost always sharper context and stricter review, not switching models or tools.

  • Treating Claude for Sensitive and Nuanced Business Writing as a finished answer rather than a draft to review
  • Vague prompts that don't specify audience, format, or what you want changed
  • Forgetting to give it context — the file, the brief, the constraints
  • Skipping the verify step — always check numbers, dates, names, and policy claims
  • Using it for tasks where a 30-second human action would be faster

✅ DO THIS

  • Provide context — the file, the brief, the audience, the constraints
  • Iterate; don't just accept the first output
  • Always review numbers, names, dates, and policy claims
  • Treat AI as a fast draft generator, not an answer engine
  • Capture what works so you can replicate it
  • Match the model to the task — fast modes for drafts, reasoning modes for analysis

❌ AVOID THIS

  • Sending AI output without reading it end-to-end
  • Vague prompts with no audience, format, or success criteria
  • Assuming AI can see files or context it hasn't been given
  • Using AI for tasks faster done manually in under a minute
  • Treating numbers, quotes, or citations as verified without checking
  • Defaulting to the slow reasoning mode for every task

Suggested Prompts
Ready-to-use prompts you can adapt to your work

These prompts are designed to land in your real work today. Replace the bracketed placeholders with your actual context — the more specific you make them, the better the output. Treat each one as a starting brief, not a finished script.

DIFFICULT INTERNAL LETTER
Draft a letter from me to one of my direct reports. Decision: we're not extending their contract beyond June. Context: performance has been declining for 3 months despite a clear performance plan. Tone: clear, kind, no soft-pedalling. Cover next steps: final day, handover, references support. I want clarity, not euphemism.
CUSTOMER APOLOGY
We had a 6-hour outage last Thursday that affected 30% of customers. Root cause was a misconfigured deployment we caught and fixed. Draft the customer apology email: own the action, explain what happened, what we've done, and what we're changing. No 'sorry-if' constructions, no blame-shifting. Sign-off is from the CEO.
INVESTOR UPDATE — BAD QUARTER
Q3 missed plan by 18% on revenue and 30% on new logo. Reasons: sales cycle elongation in EMEA, one large customer churn. We're not pivoting strategy. Draft a 1-page update to our investors: lead with the headline numbers, show the reasons clearly, state what we're doing and not doing, and end with the next data point we'll share.
