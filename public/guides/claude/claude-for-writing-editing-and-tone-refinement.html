The Four-Element Brief
What every writing prompt needs

Claude writes dramatically better when you give it four things: context, task, format, and audience. Skip any of these and you'll get something competent but generic.

  • Context — what you're writing about, key facts, what's already happened
  • Task — what you actually want produced (not just 'write something')
  • Format — length, structure, sections, what to include or omit
  • Audience — who reads it, what they care about, what tone fits

Why the brief matters more than the model

Claude is more capable than most users get out of it. The gap is almost always brief quality — the difference between 'write me an email' (generic output) and the four-element brief (publishable draft).

  • Context — what the situation is, what's happened, what's at stake
  • Task — specific instruction: draft / edit / rewrite / tighten / expand / translate
  • Format — length, structure, what to include, what to omit
  • Audience — who reads, what they care about, their reading level, their tone expectations
03
Drafting Pattern
From blank page to working draft
  • Start with a brief that includes all four elements
  • First draft from Claude — accept it's a draft, not the final
  • Iterate by section, not by full rewrite — 'tighten the opening', 'cut the third paragraph in half'
  • Edit at the end for voice — Claude can match your voice but not invent it perfectly
04
Editing Existing Text
Where Claude shines

Editing is often Claude's best work — clearer than your draft, but still recognisably yours. The pattern: paste your draft, specify what you want changed, let Claude propose, accept selectively.

  • 'Make this tighter without losing meaning'
  • 'Rewrite for a non-technical audience'
  • 'Cut 30% — what's the lowest-value paragraph?'
  • 'Suggest 3 different openings for this piece'
  • 'Where am I being unclear?'

✅ DO THIS

  • Paste the draft and specify exactly what to change
  • Use surgical asks: 'tighten the opening' not 'improve this'
  • Cut by percentage: 'reduce this by 30% without losing meaning'
  • Ask for alternatives: 'suggest 3 different openings for this piece'
  • Ask for clarity audits: 'where am I being unclear?'
  • Iterate one section at a time — full rewrites lose your voice

❌ AVOID THIS

  • Asking for 'improvements' generically — gets generic improvements
  • Letting Claude restructure the whole piece on the first pass
  • Accepting the first edit blindly — Claude over-cuts and over-formalises
  • Mixing edit types in one prompt (tighten AND expand AND restructure)
  • Forgetting to preserve your specific phrasings where they work
  • Skipping voice review after editing — Claude can drift over multiple passes
05
Tone Matching
Making Claude sound like you

Claude can match a tone if you give it 2-3 short samples of writing in that tone. Paste examples, then ask Claude to write the new piece in that voice.

🎭 TONE IS LEARNABLE
Provide samples, not abstract descriptions. 'Friendly but professional' doesn't help. Three actual emails do. Claude reverse-engineers the voice from the examples.
  • 'Here are 3 emails I've written. Draft the next email in this voice.'
  • 'Match the tone of this company blog post.'
  • 'I want this to read like a calm engineer explaining to a frustrated PM, not like marketing copy.'

The voice-matching prompt that works

Abstract descriptions don't work. Three actual samples do. Claude reverse-engineers the voice from examples — including the things you can't articulate but want preserved.

  • Paste 3 emails / posts / drafts in the target voice
  • Specify what NOT to imitate (some samples have things you want to leave behind)
  • Mention specific moves: 'I tend to open with a question', 'I use semicolons heavily'
  • Ask Claude to draft, then verify: 'does this match the voice in the samples?'
  • For brand voice, encode in a Claude Projects custom instruction — reusable across sessions

Structural Refactoring
When the order is wrong
  • 'This piece buries the lede. Restructure so the headline finding comes first.'
  • 'Reorganise so the recommendation is up front, evidence after.'
  • 'Cut the throat-clearing — start with the substantive point.'
  • 'Rewrite as bullet points; my audience won't read paragraphs.'
📐
Restructure
'Bring the headline finding to the top. Move evidence after.' Changes order; preserves content.
✂️
Tighten
'Cut throat-clearing. Start with the substantive point.' Removes warmup.
🔄
Recompose
'Rewrite as bullet points; my audience won't read paragraphs.' Changes format entirely.
07
Honest Writing Limits
Where Claude can still go wrong
  • Quotes and citations — Claude can fabricate them; always verify
  • Specific numbers and dates — Claude rounds and approximates; check against source
  • Voice over multiple pieces — Claude drifts; check the third piece doesn't read different from the first
  • Cliché and hype — Claude defaults to corporate boilerplate; cut hard

The Anti-AI-Slop Checklist
How to make Claude's writing not sound AI
  • Cut every 'It's worth noting that...' and 'In conclusion'
  • Remove 'leverage', 'unlock', 'transformative', 'revolutionary', 'unprecedented'
  • Avoid the 'not X but Y' construction (Claude overuses it)
  • Vary sentence length deliberately — Claude defaults to a metronomic rhythm
  • Add at least one specific concrete detail per paragraph
🚫 CUT THESE PHRASES BEFORE PUBLISHING
'It's worth noting that...', 'In conclusion', 'leverage', 'unlock', 'transformative', 'revolutionary', 'unprecedented', 'in today's fast-paced world', 'navigate the landscape of'. Plus the 'not X but Y' construction. AI tells; cut hard.

AI-generated writing has tells. Knowing them is what separates writing that lands from writing that gets dismissed as 'this feels AI'. The biggest tells: 'It's worth noting that...', 'In conclusion', 'leverage', 'unlock', 'transformative', 'revolutionary'. Run an anti-slop pass on every Claude draft before publishing.

More subtle tells: the 'not X but Y' construction (overused), metronomic sentence rhythm (vary length), abstract claims without concrete details (add a specific number, name, or example per paragraph).

The 5-pass anti-slop review

Before publishing any AI-assisted writing, run these five passes. Takes ~5 minutes per piece; the difference is between 'this feels AI' and 'this reads as written'.

  • Pass 1 — cut the throat-clearing opener; start with the substantive sentence
  • Pass 2 — find and cut the hype words ('transformative', 'unlock', 'leverage')
  • Pass 3 — vary sentence length deliberately — break up metronomic rhythm
  • Pass 4 — add one concrete specific (name, number, example) per paragraph
  • Pass 5 — read aloud; cut anything that sounds like a corporate AI assistant

Common Mistakes & Fixes
What goes wrong — and how to avoid it

Most AI workflows that 'don't work' fall into one of these patterns. The fix is almost always sharper context and stricter review, not switching models or tools.

  • Treating Claude for Writing Editing and Tone Refinement as a finished answer rather than a draft to review
  • Vague prompts that don't specify audience, format, or what you want changed
  • Forgetting to give it context — the file, the brief, the constraints
  • Skipping the verify step — always check numbers, dates, names, and policy claims
  • Using it for tasks where a 30-second human action would be faster

✅ DO THIS

  • Provide context — the file, the brief, the audience, the constraints
  • Iterate; don't just accept the first output
  • Always review numbers, names, dates, and policy claims
  • Treat AI as a fast draft generator, not an answer engine
  • Capture what works so you can replicate it
  • Match the model to the task — fast modes for drafts, reasoning modes for analysis

❌ AVOID THIS

  • Sending AI output without reading it end-to-end
  • Vague prompts with no audience, format, or success criteria
  • Assuming AI can see files or context it hasn't been given
  • Using AI for tasks faster done manually in under a minute
  • Treating numbers, quotes, or citations as verified without checking
  • Defaulting to the slow reasoning mode for every task

Suggested Prompts
Ready-to-use prompts you can adapt to your work

These prompts are designed to land in your real work today. Replace the bracketed placeholders with your actual context — the more specific you make them, the better the output. Treat each one as a starting brief, not a finished script.

DRAFT FROM BRIEF
Context: [...]. Task: write a [type of piece]. Format: [length, structure]. Audience: [who, what they care about]. Tone: [voice]. Draft it, then suggest 3 alternative openings I could pick from.
EDITING PASS
Edit this draft. Goals: tighter, clearer, less corporate. Cut 25%. Flag any place I'm being unclear or hedging. Don't change my voice — keep my phrasing where it works.
TONE MATCH
Here are 3 examples of how I write [type]: [paste]. Now draft [new piece] in this voice. Don't make it sound more 'professional' than the examples — match them exactly.
