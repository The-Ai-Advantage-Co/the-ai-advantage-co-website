What is Advanced Reasoning?

A different kind of intelligence — slow thinking for hard problems

ChatGPT's standard model (GPT-4o) is optimised for speed and conversation — it produces high-quality responses very quickly. But for genuinely complex, multi-step problems, speed can come at the cost of accuracy. That's where reasoning models come in.

OpenAI's reasoning models — o1, o3, and their mini variants — are built differently. Rather than generating an answer immediately, they spend time internally exploring the problem: checking logic, considering counterarguments, and working through steps before committing to a response. They think before they speak.

⚡

GPT-4o (Standard)

Fast, conversational, excellent for writing, summarisation, brainstorming, and most everyday tasks. Generates responses in seconds.

🧮

o1 / o3 (Reasoning)

Slower, more deliberate. Excels at logic, maths, complex coding, legal analysis, and any problem requiring precise multi-step thinking.

💡
The Core Difference

GPT-4o answers like a brilliant person thinking out loud in real time. Reasoning models answer like a brilliant person who went away, thought hard, ran through edge cases, and came back with a considered position. Same intelligence — different process.

When to Use Reasoning Models

The decision framework — GPT-4o first, reasoning models when it matters

The key principle: use GPT-4o for most things, and reach for a reasoning model when a wrong answer has real consequences or when the problem genuinely requires multi-step logical precision.

Use a Reasoning Model When…

🔢

Multi-Step Maths or Logic

Any calculation, proof, or logical chain where GPT-4o has been unreliable. Reasoning models dramatically reduce arithmetic and logic errors.

💻

Complex Coding Problems

Debugging intricate bugs, designing architecture, optimising algorithms, or writing code where correctness is non-negotiable.

⚖️

Legal & Contract Analysis

Spotting ambiguities, identifying clauses that conflict, or checking a contract against specific requirements — precision matters here.

🔬

Scientific & Technical Reasoning

Interpreting research findings, evaluating methodology, checking statistical reasoning, or exploring technical hypotheses.

♟️

Strategic Analysis with Trade-offs

Business decisions with many interdependent variables — market entry, resource allocation, risk analysis across multiple scenarios.

🧩

Verifying GPT-4o's Work

When GPT-4o gave you an answer but you're not sure it's right — use a reasoning model to check its logic independently.

Keep Using GPT-4o For…

TaskWhy GPT-4o Is Better Here
Writing, editing, copywritingSpeed and fluency matter more than logical precision
Brainstorming and ideationVolume and variety of ideas beats deliberate analysis
SummarisationFast and accurate enough; reasoning models add no value
Casual Q&A and researchConversational pace, factual recall — no deep reasoning needed
Image generation and multimodal tasksReasoning models don't generate images

💡
The Quick Test

Ask yourself: "If this answer is wrong, does it matter?" If yes — use a reasoning model. If it's a draft you'll review anyway, GPT-4o is fine and much faster.

04

How They Think Differently

Inside the reasoning process — what's happening before you see an answer

When you send a prompt to a reasoning model, it doesn't immediately generate an answer. Instead, it produces a hidden internal "thinking" process — a chain of reasoning tokens you don't see directly. This is what makes reasoning models qualitatively different from GPT-4o.

Example: Reasoning Trace for a Logic Problem

Internal Reasoning Thinking • ~45 seconds
The question asks whether the company can terminate without cause under clause 14.2 given the 90-day notice requirement in clause 8.1. Let me re-read both clauses carefully...
Clause 14.2 says "either party may terminate for any reason." Clause 8.1 requires 90 days written notice "for any termination under this agreement." These appear to operate together — 14.2 grants the right, 8.1 governs the process.
But wait — is there a conflict between 14.2 and the definition of "cause" in Schedule A? Let me check... Schedule A defines cause as "material breach, insolvency, or fraud." This is separate from the 14.2 without-cause right. No conflict.
What are the consequences of terminating without cause? Clause 16.3 specifies a termination fee equal to 3 months' fees. Confirming: yes, without-cause termination is permitted, 90 days notice required, termination fee applies.
✦ Final Answer
Yes, the company can terminate without cause under clause 14.2. You must provide 90 days written notice (clause 8.1) and pay a termination fee equal to 3 months' fees (clause 16.3). There is no conflict between these clauses — they operate sequentially.
💡
You Can See a Summary of the Thinking

ChatGPT shows a collapsed "Thought for X seconds" indicator you can expand. You won't see the full raw reasoning tokens, but you can see a summarised account of the reasoning process — useful for checking that the model addressed your actual question.

What This Means Practically

More Robust to Ambiguity

Reasoning models are better at identifying when a question is ambiguous and either resolving it or asking for clarification, rather than confidently answering the wrong question.

Self-Corrects Mid-Reasoning

The model actively checks its own intermediate conclusions. If step 3 contradicts step 1, it backtracks — something GPT-4o rarely does on a single generation pass.

Slower But More Reliable

Expect 15 seconds to 2+ minutes for complex problems. The wait is the model working — this is a feature, not a bug.

Less Conversational

Reasoning models are more formal and precise in tone. They're optimised for accuracy, not warmth. For conversational tasks, GPT-4o still reads better.

05

Prompting for Reasoning Models

Different rules apply — less coaching, more clarity

The prompting techniques that improve GPT-4o outputs don't always translate to reasoning models. Reasoning models handle the "how to think" internally — your job is to state the problem clearly and completely, then get out of the way.

Key Differences vs Prompting GPT-4o

TechniqueWith GPT-4oWith Reasoning Models
Role prompting ("You are an expert…")Helpful — improves tone and framingLess necessary — model already reasons at expert level
Chain-of-thought ("think step by step")Significantly improves accuracyRedundant — the model already reasons step-by-step internally
Problem statement lengthMedium — context helpsLonger is better — give all relevant information upfront
Format specificationImportant — specify clearlyStill important — specify output structure explicitly
Constraints and edge casesList if knownInclude exhaustively — the model will check against them
Iterative refinementCommon workflowLess needed — aim to get it right in one well-formed prompt

The Reasoning Model Prompt Formula

o3Prompt Structure
1. State the problem completely
Give all relevant context. Don't assume the model will ask follow-ups — it will work with what you give it.

2. Specify constraints and requirements
List every constraint explicitly. "The solution must work within X, cannot assume Y, must handle edge case Z."

3. Define the output format
"Produce: [specific format]. Include: [specific elements]. Avoid: [specific elements]."

4. Ask for reasoning (optional but useful)
"Show your reasoning process for each key conclusion." — helps you audit the answer.
💡
Front-Load Everything

Reasoning models work best on a single well-formed prompt rather than an iterative back-and-forth. Spend 5 minutes writing a thorough prompt rather than 20 minutes in a corrective loop. The model will do the thinking — your job is to give it the complete problem.

⚠️
Don't Over-Constrain the Reasoning Process

Telling a reasoning model "first do X, then do Y, then do Z" is counterproductive — it interrupts its natural reasoning flow. Specify what you want as an output; let the model decide how to get there.

Deep Research

Multi-source research synthesis powered by o3

Deep Research is a specialised mode in ChatGPT (accessible from the model selector) that uses o3 to conduct multi-step web research — searching dozens of sources, synthesising findings, and producing a structured research report with citations. It's designed for tasks that would take a human researcher hours.

🔍

What It Does

Searches the web iteratively — following links, reading full articles, and refining its search based on what it finds. Not a single search, but a full research process.

📄

What You Get

A structured report (typically 1,000–5,000 words) with: executive summary, detailed findings, source citations, and key conclusions. Ready to use or refine.

⏱️

Time to Complete

5–30 minutes depending on complexity. Deep Research runs in the background — you can leave it running and return when it notifies you it's done.

📋

Availability

ChatGPT Plus, Team, Enterprise, and Pro. Rate-limited — typically 10–25 reports per month on Plus, more on Pro.

When to Use Deep Research

✓

Market and competitive research

Landscape analysis, competitor mapping, market sizing, industry trends — tasks that normally require hours of reading.

✓

Literature reviews

Synthesising academic or industry publications on a topic. Deep Research pulls recent sources and surfaces key findings across them.

✓

Due diligence and background research

Researching a company, person, regulation, or technology before a decision. Produces a comprehensive briefing document.

✓

Complex technical questions

"What are the current best practices for [technical topic]?" — Deep Research synthesises recent sources rather than relying on training data.

How to Structure a Deep Research Request

Deep ResearchRequest Template
Research topic: [clear, specific topic statement]

Scope: [what's in / what's out — e.g. "Focus on UK market only, 2022–present"]

Key questions to answer:
1. [specific question]
2. [specific question]
3. [specific question]

Output format: [e.g. "Executive summary + detailed sections + source list. Approx. 2,000 words."]

⚠️
Always Verify Sources

Deep Research cites its sources, but hallucination is still possible — especially for specific statistics, quotes, and dates. Treat the output as a well-organised first draft that requires spot-checking on any claims you'll act on or publish.

Advanced Techniques

Getting the most from reasoning models in complex workflows

These techniques let you use reasoning models in more sophisticated ways — combining their strengths with GPT-4o's fluency, or applying them to specific high-value use cases.

The Two-Model Workflow

Use each model for what it's best at in a single workflow:

1

GPT-4o: Gather and structure

Use GPT-4o to brainstorm, gather information, outline the problem, and produce a first draft. Fast iteration at this stage.

2

Reasoning Model: Analyse and verify

Hand the draft or problem statement to o1/o3 for logic checking, gap analysis, or precise quantitative work. Let it scrutinise what GPT-4o produced.

3

GPT-4o: Polish and communicate

Take the reasoning model's analysis back to GPT-4o for writing, formatting, and making the output audience-ready. Reasoning models can be dry — GPT-4o makes it readable.

💡
Use o3 as Your Peer Reviewer

"Here is a plan I've drafted. Play devil's advocate — identify the three most significant logical flaws or unstated assumptions, and suggest how each could be addressed." This is one of the highest-value uses of reasoning models: critical review of your own work.

High-Value Reasoning Model Use Cases

Contract Red-Flagging

"Review this contract. Identify: ambiguous clauses, obligations with no defined timeline, and any terms that are unusual or unfavourable. For each, explain the risk."

Code Architecture Review

"Review this system design. Identify: race conditions, security vulnerabilities, scalability bottlenecks, and any design patterns that will cause maintenance problems at scale."

Financial Model Audit

"Check this financial model for: incorrect formulas, circular references, assumptions that aren't stated, and sensitivity to key variable changes."

Strategic Pre-Mortem

"Assume this strategy has failed completely in 18 months. What are the five most likely causes? For each, what early warning sign would have been visible at month 3?"

Common Mistakes & Fixes

What goes wrong with reasoning models and how to address it

❌ Using a reasoning model for everything, including simple tasks
Fix: Reserve reasoning models for tasks where accuracy on complex logic is critical. For writing, summarisation, brainstorming, and most content tasks, GPT-4o is faster, equally good, and more natural. Overusing reasoning models wastes rate limits and time.
❌ Adding "think step by step" or "let's reason carefully" to reasoning model prompts
Fix: These phrases are CoT techniques designed to improve GPT-4o. Reasoning models already reason step-by-step internally — adding these instructions is redundant and can slightly interfere with the model's natural process. State the problem clearly instead.
❌ Treating reasoning model outputs as definitively correct
Fix: Reasoning models are significantly more accurate than GPT-4o on complex problems, but they're not infallible. For high-stakes decisions (legal, financial, medical), verify key claims independently. Ask the model to state its assumptions and uncertainty explicitly.
❌ Giving an incomplete problem statement and iterating
Fix: Reasoning models work best on complete, well-formed prompts. Unlike GPT-4o, where iterative refinement is a natural workflow, reasoning models benefit from a thorough upfront brief. Spend time crafting a complete problem statement rather than planning to correct mid-conversation.
❌ Expecting reasoning models to be faster than GPT-4o
Fix: Reasoning models are intentionally slower — the thinking time is the product. If a task needs an immediate response, use GPT-4o. For reasoning model tasks, set the expectation that you're waiting for a more reliable answer, not a faster one.
❌ Not using Deep Research when it's the right tool
Fix: Many users ask GPT-4o research questions that would be much better served by Deep Research — market analysis, literature synthesis, technical landscape reviews. If your research question would take a human researcher more than an hour, Deep Research is likely the right tool.

See It In Action

The same problem — GPT-4o vs o3 side by side

The most compelling way to understand reasoning models is to see how they handle a problem that trips up GPT-4o. Here's a logic puzzle from contract analysis — the kind of problem where a wrong answer has real consequences.

The Problem

Scenario
A contract states: "Invoices are due 30 days from receipt. Late invoices incur 1.5% monthly interest. If payment is made within 10 days of the invoice date, a 2% early payment discount applies." An invoice is received on Jan 1. Payment is made on Jan 8. Is any interest owed? Does the discount apply?

GPT-4o Response (typical)

GPT-4o
✦ GPT-4o
No interest is owed because payment was made on Jan 8, which is within the 30-day window. The early payment discount of 2% applies because payment was made within 10 days of the invoice date.

o3 Response

o3Thinking • 22 seconds
✦ o3
Interest: No interest is owed. Payment on Jan 8 is within the 30-day payment window (due Jan 31), so the late payment clause is not triggered.

Discount: This requires attention. The contract says the discount applies "within 10 days of the invoice date" — not receipt date. If "invoice date" equals the date the invoice was issued (which may differ from Jan 1 receipt), the 10-day window runs from that issue date.

If the invoice was dated Jan 1 (same as receipt): discount applies — Jan 8 is day 7.
If the invoice was dated before Jan 1 (e.g. Dec 28): the 10-day window may have already passed by receipt date.

Recommendation: Confirm the invoice's stated date before claiming the discount. If it's Jan 1, the 2% discount applies.
💡
What Just Happened

GPT-4o gave a confident answer that was mostly correct but missed a critical ambiguity — "invoice date" vs "receipt date." o3 caught the distinction, mapped out both scenarios, and gave a conditional answer with a clear next step. In a real contract dispute, that distinction could matter significantly.
